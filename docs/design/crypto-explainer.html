sigil_ · How the security works

Two halves. One lock. Neither half can open it alone.

A plain-language tour of how Sigil lets you approve a secret from your phone, and why even a hijacked Mac cannot help itself to one. No prior knowledge assumed.

Read top to bottom. Each idea is built from the one before it.

What Sigil is

Your secrets, approved from your pocket

Passwords and access tokens live locked on your Mac. Sigil lets you approve their use from your phone while keeping them locked to everyone else, including a Mac that has been taken over.

Where we are going

From padlocks to a split key

We start with what a key even is, build up to a lock that needs two halves to open, and finish with the honest limits no design can remove.

01

The problem

A secret you cannot reach, and a Mac you cannot fully trust

Think of a secret as a key to something valuable: a password, or an access token that lets a tool talk to 1Password, a database, or a server.

Today that secret lives on your Mac, locked behind your fingerprint. Every time a tool wants it, your Mac asks you to press the sensor to unlock it. That works beautifully when you are sitting at the machine. It falls apart in two ways.

You are not always there. When you step away, the fingerprint prompt just sits there waiting. The tool that needed the secret stalls: an assistant stops mid task, a server refuses to start, a connection hangs, all because nobody is present to press the sensor.

Worse, the Mac itself could be turned against you. If a piece of malware sneaks onto your Mac, it is standing right next to the locked secret. The moment that secret is unlocked, malware nearby could try to grab it.

So we want two things at once, and they pull in opposite directions. We want to approve from your phone when you are away. And we want it so that even a compromised Mac cannot unlock a secret by itself, no matter what is running on it.

02

The one bit of cryptography you need

Padlocks, keys, and locked boxes

Everything that follows rests on two simple ideas. If you get these two, you get the whole design.

Idea one: a key that comes in a matched pair. Imagine a special padlock and the single key that opens it. You can hand out copies of the open padlock to anyone. They can snap it shut on a box, but they cannot open it back up. Only you, holding the one key, can. The padlock you share is called your public key. The key you keep is your private key. Sharing the padlock gives nothing away, because a padlock cannot be run backwards into its key.

This lets two people who have only ever exchanged padlocks send each other locked boxes that nobody in between can open.

Idea two: turning a secret into a locked box. Encryption is a machine that takes something readable, like postgres://admin:hunter2@db, plus a key, and scrambles it into meaningless noise. Without the right key the noise stays noise, forever. Feed the key back in and the exact original comes out. A stolen locked box is just gibberish to whoever holds it.

That is the entire toolkit. A padlock anyone can click shut but only you can open, and a way to scramble a secret into noise that only the right key unscrambles. Hold onto both.

03

The root of trust

Meeting in person, so no imposter can slip in

Before your Mac and phone can trade locked boxes, each has to be certain it holds the other's real padlock, and not a fake one swapped in by someone in the middle.

Sigil solves this the way two people would in real life: they meet face to face once. Your Mac shows a QR code on its screen. Your phone reads it with its camera. That hop, from a screen to a lens, has no internet in it. Nobody on the network can reach in between a screen and a camera pointed at it. So it is a trustworthy way to hand over the Mac's real padlock.

The phone sends its own padlock back over the network. Now here is the danger. The network is a crowd of strangers. Someone could intercept the phone's reply and substitute their own padlock, tricking the Mac into trusting the wrong device. This is called a person in the middle.

The six words are how you catch that. Once both devices think they have swapped padlocks, each independently boils the two padlocks it holds down to six short, memorable words, and shows them on screen. If an imposter slipped a fake padlock into the exchange, the two devices are holding different sets of padlocks, so their six words will not match. You read the words off both screens. If they agree, no imposter got in. If they differ, you stop. The check is that simple, and that is the point: a mismatch is loud and a human cannot miss it.

04

The big idea

One lock that needs two half-keys

Here is the move that makes a compromised Mac safe. The lock on your secret is not opened by a single key. It is opened only when two separate half-keys are brought together.

First, what this chapter is and is not about. Sigil does two different jobs and only one of them involves a lock. Some tools fetch their own secrets and have their own way of proving who they are; the op command is the everyday example. For those, Sigil holds nothing at all. It stands in front of the command and decides whether it runs, and if you approve, the tool goes and does exactly what it always did. There is no half-key in that story because there is nothing of yours to unlock. The split key below is about the other job: the values you hand to Sigil to keep, which it encrypts and later puts into an approved command's environment. Keep the two apart as you read, because the guarantees are not the same.

One half is held by your Mac. The other half lives only inside a special security chip in your phone, called the Secure Enclave. Think of that chip as a sealed vault with no door: things can be computed inside it, but the half-key it guards can never come out. Not to an app, not to you, not to anyone who steals the phone.

Neither half opens anything on its own. The Mac's half alone is useless. The phone's half alone is useless. Only combined do they form the key that unscrambles the secret. This is why a Mac riddled with malware still cannot unlock a secret by itself: it is holding half of a key, and half a key opens nothing.

Compare it to the older, simpler approach, where a single whole key does the unlocking. Steal that one key once and you can open every secret, forever, with no one the wiser. Splitting the key removes that single point of failure entirely. Use the toggle to see the difference.

05

The heart of it

Your face makes the key, fresh, every single time

There is no master key sitting anywhere waiting to be used. Each time a tool needs a secret, the unlock is created from scratch, and your face is what creates it.

When a tool asks for a secret, your Mac computes its own half and then asks your phone for the other half. Your phone shows you exactly what is being requested. When you approve with Face ID, and only then, the sealed chip computes its half for that one request. That half travels back, the Mac combines the two halves into the key, unscrambles the secret for that single command, and then immediately wipes it from memory.

Nothing lingers. The two halves for this request cannot be reused for the next one. By default the next request starts the whole dance over: a new ask, a new glance, a new tap of your face, a new half computed fresh in the chip. Your face is not flipping a permission switch, it is supplying a missing piece of the math for this exact command.

Unless you deliberately open a window, which you can, and which you should understand before you use it. Answering thirty prompts in an hour is the kind of friction that makes people turn a safety feature off, so Sigil offers a bounded alternative called a lease. A rule has to permit it in advance; the default is that it does not. When a rule does permit it, the approval screen offers "approve for the next N minutes" alongside the ordinary one-command approve, and if you choose it, anything that rule matches runs inside that window without asking again. Not just the command you were looking at: anything the same rule covers, started by the same chain of programs. For a value Sigil keeps, the window also holds that value unscrambled in memory the whole time, which is the price of not doing the unlock dance again.

That is a real widening of the boundary, and the design does not pretend otherwise. What keeps it bounded: you choose it, and never by accident. The Mac clamps the length down to the ceiling the rule sets, whatever the phone asks for. sigil lease list shows every open window, its countdown, and what it covers, in the same words the approval screen used. sigil lease revoke closes one immediately. Expiry, a restart of the daemon, or an edit to the rule closes it too. And nothing about a window is ever written to disk, so it cannot outlive the process that holds it.

06

A subtle, important point

Your phone never sees the secret itself

It is natural to assume the secret travels to your phone for approval. It does not. The phone never receives the password, the token, or the unscrambled value. Not once.

What the phone receives is more like a math puzzle: a meaningless number tied to this request. Inside its sealed vault, the phone does one calculation with its half-key and that number, and sends back the answer, which is just another number. That answer is the phone's half of the unlock. The actual secret stays on the Mac the entire time, and the combining happens there.

So even if your phone were completely taken over, the attacker would find no secrets on it to steal. There are none there. The phone is a hand that turns half of a lock. It never holds what is behind the lock.

07

Making it work over the internet

A postman who cannot read the mail, or forge it

When you are away from home, your Mac and phone are not on the same network. Their messages have to travel through a middleman server called a relay. The trick is to need no trust in that middleman at all.

Every message between the two is a sealed envelope. It is locked with the recipient's padlock, so only the true recipient can open it, and it carries a tamper-proof wax seal, a signature that proves it genuinely came from the other device and was not altered. The relay carries these envelopes back and forth and that is all it can do.

It cannot read what is inside, because it lacks the key. It cannot forge a message, because it cannot fake the seal. Resending an old envelope to trick a device is caught by two checks: every envelope carries the time it was written, and one older than 150 seconds is refused outright; and inside that window each envelope's serial number may be accepted exactly once.

Be precise about that second check, because it is the one with a limit. Both the clock check and the list of serial numbers already used live in each device's memory, not on its disk. Restart the daemon, or let the phone rebuild its session, and the list starts empty again. So a captured envelope replayed across a restart, and still inside its 150 seconds, would clear both checks. What stops it there is a third property that needs no memory at all: every envelope is locked with a throwaway key made fresh for that one exchange, so an old reply simply cannot be opened against a later request. That is the honest shape of it. The relay is a postman who ferries locked mail he can neither open nor counterfeit, and the mail is built so that yesterday's letter fits no lock today.

08

Putting it together

What all of this actually guarantees

Four plain promises fall out of the split key, the sealed chip, and the blind relay.

Approve from anywhere

You answer requests from your phone whether you are at your desk, on the couch, or in another city. The work no longer stalls waiting for you to be physically present.

A stolen, powered-off Mac is gibberish

At rest the Mac holds only scrambled secrets and one useless half-key. Without the phone's half, there is nothing to unscramble. A thief with your laptop gets noise.

A hijacked Mac cannot help itself

Malware on the Mac holds only half a key. It cannot produce a secret on its own; it can only ask your phone, and every answer needs your face. The exception you chose yourself is a lease: while a window you opened is still running, a matching request inside it is answered without asking. Close it with sigil lease revoke, or restart the daemon, and asking resumes.

The phone knows nothing about your secrets

The phone only ever does a piece of math with a number. It never receives, stores, or displays the secret value, so a compromised phone has nothing to leak.

09

Told straight

The honest limits, not hidden

No design is magic, and pretending otherwise would be the real security flaw. Here are the four limits Sigil does not remove, explained plainly.

One brief moment in the open

When a tool finally runs, it genuinely needs the secret to do its job, so for that instant the secret exists unscrambled on the Mac. This is unavoidable: a secret nobody can ever read is a secret nobody can ever use. Sigil keeps that window as short as physically possible and wipes it immediately after.

A window you open stays open until it closes

A lease trades some of the per-command guarantee for far less friction, and it is a trade, not a free lunch. While it runs, anything the same rule covers is approved without asking, and a kept value sits unscrambled in memory for the duration. It is opt-in per rule, you choose the length within a ceiling the rule sets, sigil lease list shows every open one, and sigil lease revoke, an expiry, a restart, or a rule edit ends it. None of it touches the disk.

Not every approval uses the chip

The split key and the sealed chip protect the values Sigil keeps for you. Gating a tool that fetches its own secrets, which is what happens with op, has no key to split, so that approval is authorised by the biometric on your phone alone. The biometric is genuine and there is no passcode fallback behind it, but the honest statement is that someone who can pass your face or thumb on your unlocked phone can approve a gated command. Losing the phone is a real event, not a shrug.

An approved request does deliver that secret

If someone has fully taken over your Mac and, at that same moment, you approve a request on your phone, that one secret is used for that one command. Sigil gates the release; it cannot police what an already-approved command then does with it.

Which is why your attention is the real last line of defence. Your phone shows you exactly what is being asked, from which program, on which machine, before you approve. If a request appears that you did not expect or did not start, declining it is the whole defence, and declining is always a single calm tap. Sigil is built so that the safe choice is the easy one.

The shape of it

Cryptography doing the work, not a promise

Notice what Sigil never asks you to trust: not the network, not the relay server, not even the Mac by itself. The safety does not rest on a company keeping a promise or a server behaving. It rests on the plain fact that half a key opens nothing, and that the phone's half is created fresh, by your face, for the request in front of you or for a window you deliberately chose to open.

Public key (padlock)
A lock you can hand out freely. Anyone can click it shut; only your private key reopens it.
Private key
The one key that opens your padlock. You keep it and never share it.
Encryption
Scrambling a readable secret into noise that only the right key can turn back.
Secure Enclave
A sealed chip in your phone. It can compute with its half-key, but the half-key can never leave it.
Split key (two halves)
A lock that opens only when the Mac's half and the phone's half are combined. Neither alone works.
Blind relay
A middleman server that only ferries sealed envelopes. It cannot read, forge, or resend them.
