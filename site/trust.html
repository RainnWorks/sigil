The trust model, in full

Assume the network is hostile. The design already did.

This is the long version for the reader who does not take "trust us" for an answer. It states the mechanism, the boundary, and the residue at each layer. It is written about the generic gate, so it holds for any provider; 1Password is simply the first one wired in. Where something is not built yet, it says so.

Pairing is the root of trust

Everything downstream inherits its security from one in-person moment. There is no server in it.

Each device mints its own Ed25519 signing key and X25519 agreement key at pairing, on the device, and the private halves are never sent anywhere. They are held by the platform's protected storage, the iOS Keychain, or AndroidKeyStore-encrypted app storage on Android, and loaded into app memory to sign and open messages. One key is different: on iPhone, the share that opens a sealed secret is generated inside the Secure Enclave and cannot be exported, by Sigil or anyone else. Android has no equivalent yet, so an Android phone can approve gated commands but cannot open threshold-sealed secrets.

On the Mac, the daemon's identity key sits in a 0600 file at ~/.sigil/keystore.json, base64-encoded rather than encrypted, alongside the Mac's threshold share. The login Keychain is available instead, opt-in through SIGIL_KEYSTORE. The signed Mac app can additionally wrap that whole file under a Secure Enclave key, which is worth doing and buys exactly one thing: an offline copy of the file becomes useless, so a backup, a snapshot, or a resold disk yields nothing. It does not keep the key out of memory. The daemon is unsigned by design, and while it runs it holds that material in ordinary RAM, readable by anything running as the same user.

Read those two paragraphs precisely, because they say something narrower than they might look. An identity private key is never transmitted: it is minted on the device and stays there. But on none of the three platforms is it confined to a hardware security element; on all three it is loaded into ordinary process memory to do its work. This page used to claim the stronger thing. Exactly one key in this product is genuinely hardware-bound and non-exportable, and it is the iPhone threshold share.

The public keys cross through a QR code rendered locally on one screen and read by the other camera, then confirmed by a six-word fingerprint that both screens display and you compare by eye. Once confirmed they are pinned forever. From that point every message in both directions is a NaCl-style sealed box (X25519 with XSalsa20-Poly1305) carrying an Ed25519 signature over pinned keys. A compromised network hop can neither read a request nor forge an approval.

Sigil holds no credential for a command that fetches its own. That is what op is: the shim gates the call, the real op runs and authenticates itself exactly as before, and Sigil never sees its token. What Sigil does hold are the values you seal into it yourself, and those are encrypted at rest under a key that exists in no one place. It is reconstructed for each approval from two shares: one the Mac keeps, and one that is computed inside the phone's Secure Enclave and never leaves it. The Mac's share opens nothing on its own.

That split is also what separates the two approval paths, and the difference is worth stating precisely. Approving a plain gate (every op rule today, and the shipping primary path) is authorised by a biometric on the phone and nothing else; no enclave key is used, because there is nothing to decrypt. Approving a sealed value additionally requires the enclave to compute that request's share, which is a hardware operation the phone cannot fake or delegate. On a paired install there is no Mac-local approval path to compare against: the daemon resolves exactly one approving factor when it arms, a pairing outranks the host biometric, and an approve issued at the Mac can only answer a request the Mac itself parked, which a phone-bound request never is. The Mac biometric is the factor only on a daemon with no phone paired at all.

Now put the phone's two custodies against that split, because the consequence matters more than the location. Anyone who obtains that stored identity, by reading it off the device or by restoring an encrypted backup onto another phone, can act as this phone toward your Mac: read the approval requests it receives, and approve any gated command. They cannot open a threshold-sealed secret, because the share that does that is generated inside this iPhone's Secure Enclave and cannot be copied.

Read that at its real strength. It is not only a forging capability: the agreement key is what opens the requests sealed to this phone, so it also discloses the command lines, the process provenance, and the secret references your Mac has been sending. And it does not require the biometric or the enclave, because neither guards the identity keys. What the enclave buys is the second half of the sentence, and that half is real.

Since this was written, the phone stores that record with a device-only accessibility class intended to keep it out of encrypted backups, with a migration for existing installs. It is not reflected above because it has not been verified on hardware: the test that settles it is to pair, take an encrypted backup, restore onto a second device, and confirm the restored app is unpaired. Until someone runs that, assume the backup path is open.

What each party can never read

The boundary is not a policy. It is what the cryptography structurally allows each party to hold.

Read the last row honestly. An approved request does deliver that secret to that process. Sigil gates release; it does not sandbox the recipient. The defense against a malicious caller is that every fulfillment needs a fresh, out-of-band approval that shows you the process and its working directory before anything is unsealed.

The enumerated worst case of a malicious relay

The relay is the one component you might not run yourself, so it gets the harshest treatment: assume it is fully adversarial and owned by someone who wants your secrets.

The relay is a ciphertext mailbox. The daemon connects to it outbound over a WebSocket and the phone reaches it the same way; it stores and forwards opaque envelopes. Because you cannot prove what a remote server runs, the design refuses to depend on its behaviour at all. Unlike a messaging app, it does not even distribute keys: pairing happened out-of-band by QR, so there is no key-exchange step for it to sit in the middle of.

Its entire possible behaviour is a short, closed list. Each entry is either harmless or fails closed, and each is enforced in client code you can read and build.

Everything it can do

  • Deliver. The intended path. Verified end to end anyway.
  • Drop or delay. A denial of service. No envelope reaches the phone, so no approval returns, so no secret is released. Fails closed.
  • Duplicate or reorder. Two gates reject the replay: a 150-second freshness window on the sender's timestamp, and a set of request ids that may each be accepted once. Behind both, a replayed response still names the request it answered, and the daemon drops any response it has no live waiter for.
  • Observe. Mailbox address, message timing, blob sizes. This is the real residue.

Everything it cannot do

  • Read a request or an approval. Both are sealed to keys it does not hold.
  • Forge either one. Both are signed by a key pinned at pairing.
  • Learn a secret name, account, command, or value.
  • Identify you. It holds two pseudonymous public keys and nothing else: no account, no email, no push token, because the daemon sends pushes directly to Apple or Google.

The replay gates are worth stating with their residual. Both live in memory on each end, so a restart of the daemon or a new phone session starts with an empty set of seen ids. An envelope captured and replayed inside its 150-second window across that restart would clear both gates. What stops it is that the response still names the request it answered, and after a restart there is no longer anything waiting on that request, so it is dropped. For lease control the phone does the same thing in its own direction, applying a reply only if it matches an unanswered request from this session. That correlation is a gate the two ends had to build deliberately, and it is the one carrying the restart case.

The per-request ephemeral key is doing something else, and the two are easy to confuse because they sound alike. It gives forward secrecy against compromise of the sender's long-term key: traffic recorded today stays unreadable if that key falls tomorrow. It is not a replay defence, because a captured envelope carries its own ephemeral and opens perfectly well. And it does nothing for the other direction: a stolen recipient agreement key still opens everything sealed to it, recordings included, which is why the phone's stored identity is as consequential as the section above says. A per-pairing monotonic counter used to be a third replay gate and was deliberately retired: a restart reset it on either side and it began dropping genuine, human-approved envelopes as false replays.

The mailbox address is a BLAKE2 hash of the two paired public keys: self-authenticating, squat-proof, and anonymous. The relay's only check, dropping envelopes not signed by one of those two keys, is anti-abuse and never load-bearing. The client verifies everything itself and would be equally safe against a relay that performed no checks at all. Self-host the Worker or the protocol-identical Bun server to keep even the metadata at home.

Claim, code, test

A security claim you cannot check is marketing. Sigil is built so each one is a line you can follow.

The protocol core lives in crates/sigil-proto: the sealed and signed envelope, the pairing and fingerprint logic, the replay guard, the threshold combine, and the mailbox hash. It has no I/O and no platform code, so it is exhaustively testable in isolation. That is where the proof lives too.

The repository ships a deliberately hostile relay implementation whose whole job is to attack: it tampers with envelopes, replays old ones, reorders them, forges signatures, truncates ciphertext, and stalls. A client test suite runs the full protocol across that hostile relay and asserts that every attack is rejected and that every failure is closed, never open. Alongside it is a claim map: each sentence of the trust model points at the function that enforces it and the test that exercises the attack it defends against.

None of this is aspirational scaffolding. It is the part being built first, because a protocol you cannot attack in CI is a protocol you are only hoping is correct.

Why client-side and not audited-server

An audit tells you what a server ran on the day of the audit. Client-side proof tells you what any server, audited or not, honest or not, is structurally unable to do. Sigil chooses the second because it is the only one that survives the server being replaced, compromised, or lying.

The residual risks, stated plainly

Four things are true that no amount of cryptography removes. A security tool that hid them would be lying.

A lease approves things you have not seen yet
A rule can be marked leasable, and then one approval may open a window instead of answering one command. Inside that window anything the rule matches, from anywhere on this Mac, runs without asking again, and for a sealed source the unsealed values are held in RAM for its duration. That is a real widening and it is the honest price of not tapping thirty times an hour. What bounds it: the default is run-once, a lease only exists when the rule allows it and you chose it, the daemon clamps the duration to the rule's cap, sigil lease list shows every live window with its countdown and what it covers, sigil lease revoke ends one immediately, and a restart, an expiry, or an edit to the rule kills it. Nothing is written to disk.
An approved consumer gets the secret
Sigil decides whether a secret is released, not what the caller does with it afterward. If you approve a request, that process receives that secret in cleartext on its stdout, exactly as if you had clicked the Mac prompt. The protection is that you saw the process, the directory, and the item first, and that nothing is released without that fresh decision.
The relay sees metadata
A relay you do not host can observe that some pairing has traffic, when, and roughly how large. It cannot tie that to a name, an email, or an Apple identifier, and it cannot read a byte of content. Self-hosting the relay in your own account removes even the metadata from anyone else's view.
Push is the irreducible platform dependency
iOS offers no way to remotely wake an app except APNs, and Android none except FCM, so the doorbell is the one piece that must go through a platform vendor, and pushing on iOS requires the paid Developer Program. Apple sees "Approval requested" and nothing more; the informative text is assembled on-device after the app fetches the sealed request down the ladder. If the phone has no path to the Mac at all, requests fail closed: approve at the Mac with Touch ID, or let it time out and fall back to vanilla op. Never a bypass.
